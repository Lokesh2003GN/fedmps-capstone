# CHAPTER 1 | Introduction

Thousands of people were reported missing every year, and the first hours after a disappearance were the most valuable for finding them. Cities already operated large networks of CCTV cameras in railway stations, bus terminals, markets and streets, so a person who walked past several of these cameras left a trail that could in principle be searched automatically. Modern deep face-recognition models compared a photograph of a missing person against the faces seen by a camera in milliseconds, which meant that the technical ingredients of an automated search already existed. The obstacle was not accuracy alone but privacy, and this chapter described that problem, the objectives of the project, the surveyed literature and the background on which FedMPS was built.

The straightforward way to search many cameras was to collect all footage, or all detected faces, on one central server and to run face recognition there. That design copied the faces of every bystander who passed any camera into one database and created a single point of failure whose compromise would have exposed the movements of an entire city. It also conflicted with data-protection principles such as data minimisation and purpose limitation, and camera operators belonging to different organisations were often unwilling, or not permitted, to hand over their footage. This project therefore asked whether camera sites could cooperate on a missing-person search, and even improve a shared recognition model, without sharing raw video, face crops or unprotected biometric data. It combined three ideas. FL trained a shared model by exchanging only model updates, privacy mechanisms such as SA, DP and cancelable templates protected those updates and the watchlist, and a human-in-the-loop review required a person to confirm every match before anything left a camera site. The system was implemented end to end, from dataset preparation on an AWS GPU instance to a working Streamlit application, and every claim made about it was measured.

## 1.1 Problem Statement

The problem was stated as follows. Given a photograph of a missing person and a set of camera sites, called nodes, each holding its own video footage, the system had to flag the camera frames in which the missing person appeared at a false-alarm rate low enough that a human operator could review every alert. It had to keep each site's raw video, the faces of uninvolved people and the site's training data on that site. It had to avoid sending the missing person's photograph, or a reusable biometric template of it, to the sites in a form that could be leaked, linked across sites or reused for another purpose. It had to let the sites improve a shared recognition model for their own camera conditions without pooling data, and it had to record who saw what and who decided what, so that every disclosure could be audited. Two properties made the problem hard. First, CCTV faces were small, blurred, compressed and badly lit, so recognition accuracy dropped sharply compared with clean photographs. Second, the task was open-set, because almost everyone a camera saw was on no watchlist at all and the system had to stay silent for them. Accuracy on a closed set of enrolled people, which most published work reported, said little about whether such a system was usable in practice.

## 1.2 Objectives

The project had two objectives. The first objective was to build a multi-camera missing-person search pipeline, comprising face detection, tracking, quality gating, five-point landmark alignment and ArcFace embeddings, whose recognition model was trained by FL across camera nodes that never shared their data. The second objective was to implement pairwise-masked SA, DP on model updates and cancelable watchlist templates, and to measure the accuracy cost of each of them against the same training run without it. Both objectives were evaluated with open-set identification metrics on a large face dataset degraded to CCTV quality and on real surveillance footage, and both were demonstrated in a working application with a server console, node consoles and an audit log.

## 1.3 Literature Survey

The literature survey for this project reviewed sixteen recent works on missing-person identification, privacy-preserving biometrics and federated face recognition, and five further works on federated and privacy-preserving face recognition were selected to compare the results. The sixteen surveyed works fell into three groups, which were missing-person identification with template protection, federated face recognition, and FL applied to other facial tasks or to de-identification. Each group was reviewed for its datasets, its method, its main finding and the gap that it left open, and {tab:lit} summarised all sixteen of them in the order of the literature survey report [@lit1, @lit2, @lit3, @lit4, @lit5, @lit6, @lit7, @lit8, @lit9, @lit10, @lit11, @lit12, @lit13, @lit14, @lit15, @lit16].

The first group covered missing-person identification and template protection. Rakshika et al. detected people with YOLO and matched faces with ArcFace or FaceNet embeddings, reaching 97.50% accuracy on 780 synthetic group images, but their system decided frame by frame and they named video tracking as the open problem [@lit1]. Hayat et al. protected InsightFace features in DeepShieldFed as 96-bit keyed binary templates trained with FL and DP, and reached 99.75% accuracy on CelebA [@lit2]. Krishna Prakasha and Sumalatha surveyed biometric privacy techniques and found that each of them traded security against accuracy or latency [@lit3]. The second group covered federated face recognition. Aldahiri et al. combined split FL with partial SMPC and LDP and reached 94.68% accuracy with an MIA AUC of 0.507 to 0.532 [@lit4]. Nusa et al. found FedProx better than FedAvg on LFW data that was not IID, with accuracy falling as the DP noise grew [@lit6]. Zheng et al. replaced the server with a blockchain and reached 92.22% against 94.67% for centralised training [@lit7]. Solomon et al. showed that SA raised the EER only slightly, from 1.66% to about 2.8% [@lit10].

The third group applied FL to other facial tasks or studied de-identification. FL was applied to micro-expression recognition with HE [@lit5]. It was also applied to facial-expression recognition, including a few-shot variant [@lit8, @lit14]. Other systems used it for factory attendance and for edge video sensing [@lit9, @lit11]. Two further works combined it with blockchain-audited records and with committee-based aggregation [@lit12, @lit13]. Chriskos et al. studied face de-identification against deep CNNs [@lit15]. Kutlugün et al. studied data augmentation for small face datasets and raised accuracy from 50% to 76% [@lit16]. For the comparison of results in Section 4.11, the VGGFace2 reference model and five recent federated or privacy-preserving face-recognition systems were chosen, because they reported results on VGGFace2 or on the same verification and identification metrics that this project measured [@vggface2, @fedhide, @fedfs, @kimteoh, @dpfedface, @ji2022].

## 1.4 Technical Background

FedMPS combined techniques from distributed machine learning, face recognition, applied cryptography and biometric template protection, and it was evaluated with the metrics of open-set identification rather than closed-set accuracy. This section introduced each of these techniques in the form in which the project used it, so that the later chapters could refer to them directly. The first sub-section described FL and the two aggregation methods used, the second described face recognition with ArcFace and the cosine similarity on which every match was based, the third described SA and DP, the fourth described cancelable biometric templates and the last described the open-set metrics by which the system was judged.

### 1.4.1 FL and Its Aggregation Methods

In FL, a server repeatedly sent a global model to the participating clients, each client trained it on its own data, and the server averaged the returned models, so the training data never left the clients [@fedavg]. This weighted averaging was FedAvg, in which each client counted in proportion to the number of training examples it held. When the clients held very different data, that is data that was not IID, the local models drifted apart during local training and their average could be worse than any of them. FedProx addressed this client drift by adding a proximal term to each client's loss that penalised moving far from the global model received at the start of the round, and it was used for all federated training in this project [@fedprox]. In FedMPS each camera node was one client and the central server was the aggregator, and because every camera degraded faces in its own way the node data was strongly non-IID by construction, which was exactly the setting in which FedProx was expected to help.

### 1.4.2 Face Recognition and Cosine Similarity

ArcFace trained a deep network so that faces mapped to 512-dimensional unit vectors, called embeddings, with an additive angular margin that pulled the embeddings of the same person together on the hypersphere and pushed different people apart [@arcface]. Two faces with embeddings x and y were then compared by their cosine similarity, which was given by {eq:cos}.

$$ cos(x, y) = ( x · y ) / ( ‖x‖ ‖y‖ ) $$ {#eq:cos}

In {eq:cos}, x · y was the dot product of the two embeddings and ‖x‖ and ‖y‖ were their lengths. For unit-length embeddings the denominator was 1, so the similarity reduced to the dot product, and a value near 1 meant the same person while a value near 0 meant unrelated faces. Every match decision in FedMPS, at enrolment, in the evaluation and in the live pipeline, was a comparison of this similarity with a threshold, so the quality of the embeddings decided the accuracy of the whole system.

### 1.4.3 SA and DP

SA let a server learn the sum of the clients' updates without seeing any individual update. In the pairwise-masking construction used here, every pair of clients agreed a shared secret with Diffie–Hellman key exchange and derived a random mask from it, one client of the pair added the mask and the other subtracted it, and every mask therefore cancelled in the sum. DP bounded how much any single participant could change the output of a computation. In FL, each update was clipped to a maximum norm and Gaussian noise was added to it, and the guarantee was expressed as a privacy budget ε, where a smaller ε was more private, accounted over all rounds with the Rényi DP accountant. The two mechanisms protected different things, since SA hid each node's update from the server while DP limited what the final model could reveal about any one node's data.

### 1.4.4 Cancelable Biometrics

A cancelable biometric template was a deliberately distorted, key-dependent version of a biometric feature. Templates made with the same key still matched each other, a leaked template was revoked simply by issuing a new key, and templates made with different keys could not be linked to each other. In FedMPS the watchlist was distributed to the camera nodes only as such templates, with a different key for every case and every node, so a node never held the missing person's photograph and two nodes could not link their watchlists. The price of this protection was some loss of accuracy, because binarising an embedding discarded part of the information it carried, and that price was measured in Chapter 4.

### 1.4.5 Open-set Identification Metrics

For a watchlist search, the standard measure of false alarms was the FPIR, the fraction of people not on the watchlist who still raised an alert, and the standard measure of success was the TPIR, the fraction of watchlist people who were flagged and also correctly identified. A system was compared by its TPIR at a fixed FPIR, for example the TPIR at an FPIR of 1%, and these two rates rather than closed-set accuracy decided whether an operator could trust the alerts. Closed-set identification was reported as rank-k accuracy, the fraction of probes whose correct identity was among the k best matches. One-to-one verification was reported as the TAR at a fixed FAR, together with the AUC and the EER, which made the results comparable with the published verification work of Section 4.11.

## 1.5 Research Gaps and Contributions

The surveyed work left five gaps that this project addressed. Existing missing-person systems decided on single frames, while FedMPS raised an alert only when several frames of one track agreed. Most published results were closed-set accuracies, while FedMPS was evaluated as an open-set search with 31,850 probes of people who were not on the watchlist. The accuracy cost of privacy mechanisms was rarely measured, while FedMPS measured each mechanism against the same training run without it. Federated results were often reported without the right bounds, while FedMPS compared federated training with local-only, centralised and frozen-model baselines on identical data. Finally, the surveyed systems disclosed matches automatically, while FedMPS put human review and an audit log before any disclosure.
