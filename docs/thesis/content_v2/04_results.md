# CHAPTER 4 | Results and Discussions

This chapter presented the measured results of FedMPS. All accuracy numbers came from the fixed test split described in Section 2.3.9, and every number could be traced to a results file in the `results/` folder of the project. The main results came from the VGGFace2-HQ run of 25 October 2026, prepared and trained on the AWS GPU instance, with `experiments_vgghq.json` holding the training regimes and privacy experiments, `experiments_vgghq_base.json` holding the degradation and operating-point measurements and `pilot_vgghq.json` holding the model selection. The application test and the real-CCTV benchmark of Sections 4.8 and 4.9 used the same latest adapter, so every figure and table in this chapter described one trained model.

## 4.1 The Size of the CCTV Problem and the Operating Point

The CCTV degradation was severe and face alignment mattered. Measured on the prepared embeddings, the average cosine similarity between two images of the same person fell from 0.509 when both were clean to 0.264 when a clean image was compared with a degraded one, while between different people it stayed near zero at 0.006 and 0.015 respectively. Alignment had been the single largest improvement in the project, since in a controlled A/B test during the earlier run on the original VGGFace2, with the same 5,000 identities, seed and degradation in both arms, it had raised the average same-person cosine similarity from 0.292 to 0.514, lowered the cross-person similarity from 0.103 to 0.004 and raised rank-1 at the 3 × 3 operating point from 0.206 to 0.985, which was why it was applied to every image in the final run.

The degradation model made recognition much harder. With the frozen backbone and plain cosine matching at the default operating point of three enrolment photographs by three fused frames, rank-1 fell from 0.9989 on clean probes to 0.9683 on CCTV-degraded probes, and the TPIR at an FPIR of 1% fell from 0.9912 to 0.8744. With one photograph and one frame the drop was far larger, from 0.8908 to 0.6621. With the scoring layer added, the frozen backbone reached a rank-1 of 0.9733 at the default operating point on the 691-person watchlist, and that was the baseline against which every trained model in this chapter was compared.

{fig:oppoints} showed how accuracy depended on the number of enrolment photographs and fused frames and on the scoring layer. Fusing frames and averaging several enrolment photographs mattered far more than any learned correction, since going from one photograph and one frame, with a rank-1 of 0.6710, to three photographs and three frames, with 0.9733, added about 31 rank-1 points. Fusing frames was also worth more than adding photographs, because one photograph with three frames reached 0.8977 while three photographs with one frame reached only 0.7744. At five photographs and five frames rank-1 reached 0.9976. The scoring layer added a further 0.2 to 0.9 points at every operating point, with centring, rotation and the hubness correction each contributing a part, as the lower graph of {fig:oppoints} showed for the default operating point.

## 4.2 Model Selection by the Pilot

The pilot of Section 2.3.4 trained every candidate for 30 federated rounds on 90% of the training identities and scored it on the other 10%, 321 people whose 160-person gallery and 7,044 mate probes were drawn only from the training 70%, while the test identities were not used. The frozen backbone reached a TPIR of 0.9299 at an FPIR of 1% and an EER of 0.1113. The residual MLP adapter with 64 hidden units and μ = 3, the setting of the earlier run, reached 0.9371 and 0.1106 with an update of 0.26 MB. The MLP adapter with 256 hidden units and μ = 0.3 reached the highest TPIR of 0.9390 and the lowest EER of 0.1087 with an update of 1.05 MB, and it was chosen. The three head adapters, which changed the backbone's own last layers, reached TPIRs between 0.9327 and 0.9363 and EERs between 0.1096 and 0.1113 while costing 1.64 MB per update, so they were no better than the MLP adapter on the backbone's output and cost 1.6 times the traffic. Every trained candidate left rank-1 unchanged at about 0.986, and every gain was already below one point at this stage, which anticipated the main result.

The federated adapter was ahead of the frozen backbone on every open-set and verification metric, by 0.53 points of TPIR at an FPIR of 1%, 1.04 points at 0.01%, 0.66 points of TAR at a FAR of 1% and 0.33 points of EER, while rank-1 was unchanged. The gain was consistent but small, since with 30,540 mate probes the 95% sampling band of the TPIR at 1% was about ±0.32 points, so the main gain lay just outside one band and inside two. Before the run, a gain of at least 2 points of TPIR at an FPIR of 1% had been set as the bar for recommending that the adapter be trained, and that bar was not met. Federated and centralised training were equal within the sampling noise, with federated training 0.27 points ahead at 1% and 0.01 points behind in rank-1. The federated loop warned that federated training had beaten centralised training, which could have pointed to leakage, but the identities of the nodes and of the test set were disjoint by construction and were checked when the data was partitioned, so the more likely reading was that averaging five differently degraded clients acted as a mild regulariser. Keeping the data at the camera sites therefore cost no measurable accuracy compared with pooling it.

Against local-only training, collaboration clearly mattered. Every node's local adapter was worse than the federated one and worse than the frozen backbone, by 1.1 rank-1 points and 2.4 TPIR points on average, so the federated adapter was 1.14 rank-1 points and 2.95 TPIR points above the local mean. The smallest node, node 0, with 322 people and the harshest camera, lost most, reaching a rank-1 of 0.9472 and a TPIR of 0.8467, 2.6 rank-1 points below the frozen backbone, while the largest node, node 4, with 969 people, reached 0.9689 and 0.8961 and still did not recover the frozen accuracy. A site that fine-tuned alone on its own few hundred people under one camera learned that camera's people rather than a correction that transferred to new people. In the earlier run, a fairness check with the script `training/03_check_local_only.py` had shown that early stopping and a proximal penalty brought a local adapter back to the frozen accuracy but no higher, so the two runs together showed that a site working alone gained nothing from training and could lose by it, while federation turned the same data into a small gain. The clean ceiling, a TPIR of 0.9912 at an FPIR of 1%, was far above every degraded arm at about 0.91, so most of the difficulty lay in the camera degradation and not in the choice of training regime.

## 4.4 Convergence over 200 Rounds

{fig:convergence} showed the rank-1 and the TPIR at an FPIR of 1% of the federated and centralised adapters after every one of the 200 rounds, with the frozen backbone as a dashed line and the 95% sampling band of the federated curve shaded.

![Rank-1 and TPIR at 1% FPIR over 200 rounds with the 95% sampling band](figures/fig12_convergence_200.png){#fig:convergence w=6.0}

The curves answered the instability seen in the earlier run, whose validation rank-1 had still risen and fallen after 30 rounds. Here the federated TPIR was above the frozen backbone from round 1 and stayed between 0.9083, its lowest at round 30, and 0.9112, its highest at round 145, and the last round, which was the one reported, gave 0.9107. Over the last 20 rounds the standard deviation was 0.003 points for the TPIR and 0.002 points for rank-1, and the norm of the aggregated update fell from 0.178 in round 1 to 0.00002 in round 200, so the model had settled. The cosine learning-rate schedule was what made it settle, because the late rounds could only make very small changes. Rank-1 stayed between 0.9728 and 0.9744 throughout, inside the sampling band around the frozen value, while the centralised adapter started below the frozen backbone, at a TPIR of 0.9038 in round 18, and recovered to 0.9080. The federated, centralised and local-only arms took 1,212 s, 1,203 s and 232 s of GPU time. Passing over all of a node's data in every round had been tried first and had overfitted, because after 200 full epochs the local-only adapters fell well below the frozen backbone, which was why each round used only 15% of a node's data.

Three observations explained why the gain of the adapter was so small even with 136 images per person and 200 rounds. First, even pooling all data did not help more, because the centralised arm, which saw every node's data, gained no more than the federated arm, so the limit lay in what the data allowed an embedding-space correction to achieve and not in FL. Second, much of the degradation was lost information, since a face 20 to 48 pixels wide, blurred and JPEG-compressed had lost detail that no mapping of 512-dimensional embeddings could restore, and the gap of about 8 points between clean and degraded TPIR in {tab:main} was mostly of this kind. Third, the recoverable part was rigid and the scoring layer already captured it, because the camera shifted all embeddings in a similar direction, centring and a rotation captured this shift for every arm, and the adapter could only add what was left over.

## 4.5 Per-camera Results

Comparing the frozen backbone and the federated adapter separately on the probes of each node's camera profile, about 6,100 mate probes per camera, showed that the cameras differed much more from each other than the two models differed on any one camera. The TPIR at an FPIR of 1% ranged from 0.8385 on node 0's camera, with faces of 21 pixels, heavy motion blur and JPEG quality 50, to 0.9633 on node 4's camera, with faces of 47 pixels and quality 66, while node 1, node 2 and node 3 reached 0.9243, 0.9245 and 0.8856 with the frozen backbone. The adapter helped most where the camera was worst, raising the TPIR on node 0 from 0.8385 to 0.8480, a gain of 0.95 points, and rank-1 from 0.9549 to 0.9562, while on node 2 it added 0.53 points, on node 1 and node 3 it changed the TPIR by less than 0.1 point and on the best camera, node 4, it lowered it slightly from 0.9633 to 0.9615. The ranking of the cameras followed face size, except that node 1, with the heaviest blur and sensor noise, fell behind node 2 despite its larger faces.

## 4.6 FedAvg against FedProx

In a sweep of 30 rounds scored with plain cosine, plain FedAvg with μ = 0 reached a rank-1 of 0.9714, and FedProx with μ = 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0 reached 0.9715, 0.9715 and 0.9714, so all settings fell within 0.0001 of each other. Client drift, which FedProx corrected, was therefore not a limiting factor here, because each round trained on only 15% of a node's data and the adapter started as the identity. The main run used μ = 0.3, chosen in the pilot, and since μ changed rank-1 by less than 0.01 point across two orders of magnitude, this choice did not affect any conclusion of the project.

## 4.7 Privacy Evaluation

The four privacy mechanisms of Sections 2.3.6 to 2.3.8 were each measured against the same training run without them, so that the accuracy cost of each could be read off directly. The privacy experiments ran for 30 rounds rather than 200 and were scored with plain cosine, without the scoring layer, so their reference was the frozen backbone's raw rank-1 of 0.9683 rather than 0.9733, and their differences, not their absolute values, carried the result. {tab:privacy} summarised the measured effect and cost of each mechanism, and the four sub-sections below gave the details of SA, DP, the membership inference test and the cancelable templates in turn.

Table: Privacy mechanisms with their measured effect and cost {#tab:privacy}

| Mechanism | Result | Cost or limit |
|---|---|---|
| SA with pairwise masks and 2048-bit Diffie–Hellman | Rank-1 0.9713 without masking and 0.9713 with masking; difference 0.0000 | Run time 3.1% longer, 92.0 s against 89.2 s; no recovery if a node drops out |
| DP with clip C = 0.313 and Gaussian noise | Rank-1 0.9707 without noise, 0.0566 at σ = 0.3 and 0.0011 at σ = 2.0 | ε = 252 at σ = 0.3; no noisy setting usable |
| MIA, loss-threshold attack on held-out images of the same people | Attack AUC 0.5010, where 0.5 is chance | One attack type only |
| Cancelable templates, keyed binarisation with XOR | Rank-1 0.9715 as float, 0.9244 at 512 bits | 4.7 rank-1 points at 512 bits |

### 4.7.1 SA

Masked and unmasked training produced an identical rank-1 of 0.9713 over 30 rounds, because the masks cancelled exactly in the sum, and the cost was 3.1% more run time. This was the clearest positive privacy result, since the server learned only the aggregate update at no cost in accuracy. In the main three-way run masking was switched off, and since masking did not change the aggregated update, switching it on would have left {tab:main} unchanged. The masks had elements of magnitude about 1, while the elements of an adapter delta were a few thousandths, and in the illustration recorded by the experiment a node's update had a norm of 5.13 and its masked version a norm of 1,026.

### 4.7.2 DP

The calibrated clip norm C = 0.313 lay between the median update norm of 0.241 and the 95th percentile of 0.399, so one update in three was clipped. The DP sweep traced the trade-off between the privacy budget and accuracy over 30 rounds at δ = 10⁻⁵. Clipping alone, with σ = 0 and therefore no formal guarantee, kept a rank-1 of 0.9707 and a verification AUC of 0.9404. With σ = 0.3, 0.6, 1.0 and 2.0 the budget ε fell to 251.9, 83.6, 40.0 and 15.9, while rank-1 fell to 0.0566, 0.0118, 0.0046 and 0.0011 and the AUC to 0.7103, 0.6062, 0.5511 and 0.5119. A meaningful guarantee needed an ε of roughly 10 or less, and here even the weakest noise destroyed the model. The noise was added to each of the 263,424 coordinates of the update, so at σ = 0.3 its norm was about σ · C · √263,424 ≈ 48 against about 0.24 for a typical update, and with only five clients the noise did not average out and swamped the adapter's residual output. Rank-1 then fell far below the frozen backbone, because the noisy adapter damaged the embeddings rather than leaving them alone. DP was therefore implemented and measured but not usable at this scale, and the earlier run, with a four times smaller adapter, had kept a rank-1 of 0.875 at σ = 0.3, which was consistent with the noise norm growing with the square root of the number of parameters.

### 4.7.3 Membership Inference

A loss-threshold MIA tried to tell whether an image had been in a node's training data from how well the model scored it. For this test, 20% of the images of every training identity were held out, the federated adapter was retrained without them, and the attack compared 87,611 training images with 87,611 held-out images of the same people. The attack AUC was 0.5010, which was indistinguishable from guessing at 0.5 and close to the 0.507 to 0.532 reported by Aldahiri et al. for their federated system [@lit4]. The earlier run had reported an AUC of 0.4963, but its held-out images had in fact been trained on, so it had not tested membership, and the corrected test replaced it. A second, identity-level test, with an AUC of 0.5763, compared training identities with identities never seen by any node, which measured whether a person was enrolled rather than trained on, so it was not comparable and was not used as evidence, and only one attack type was tested.

### 4.7.4 Cancelable Templates

The cancelable-template scheme used in the system, which combined dimension selection, mean thresholding and XOR keying, was measured at increasing bit widths on the full test set. At 32, 64, 96, 128 and 256 bits it reached a rank-1 of 0.1069, 0.2931, 0.4602, 0.5826 and 0.8138 and a TPIR at an FPIR of 1% of 0.0225, 0.0730, 0.1871, 0.2796 and 0.5757, and at 512 bits, which was 64 bytes, it reached 0.9244 and 0.7864, against 0.9715 and 0.8787 for the unprotected float embedding of 2,048 bytes. The 96-bit width used in the literature therefore lost 51 rank-1 points under CCTV degradation, because the genuine score fell below the impostor score, while at 512 bits the cost was 4.7 rank-1 points and 9.2 TPIR points, so 512 bits was the configured width. In the earlier run, a random projection before binarising had been somewhat more accurate at every width, and adopting it was a straightforward improvement left for future work. Because templates still cost accuracy, the live pipeline defaulted to cosine matching on the enrolled embedding, with template matching available as an option.

## 4.8 Operational Results of the Application

The complete case workflow was run on the team laptop with the latest trained adapter, using the recorded university event video of node 0 and a reference photograph of one person who appeared in it. The video had 1,747 frames, of which every fourth frame was processed, 437 frames in all, at 1.0 FPS on the laptop CPU. The detector found 1,892 faces, and the quality gate passed 852 of them and discarded 1,040, which was 55%, of which 1,001 were too small, 29 were turned too far away, 8 were too blurred and 2 were badly exposed. Matching produced 87 frame-level hits above the cosine threshold of 0.24, and track voting turned them into 7 track-level alerts, a reduction by a factor of 12.4.

{fig:frame} showed one frame of the node's annotated output, in which the enrolled person appeared twice in different photographs of the event and was matched in red with scores of 0.64 and 0.43, while the other faces were shown in green with scores between −0.05 and 0.15 and were not matched. The operator compared each of the 7 alerts with the reference photograph, confirmed 6 of them, whose crops showed the enrolled person, and rejected one, whose crop showed a different man with a score of 0.283 just above the threshold, so the reviewed precision at the node was 6 of 7. After the review the server showed the 6 confirmed sightings with their scores, votes, operator names and times, as {fig:sighting} showed, while the rejected alert never left the node. For the test case the log held 76 entries, namely one case creation, five template issues, seven alerts raised, six confirmations, one rejection and 56 views of the reference photograph during the review.

## 4.9 Evaluation on Real CCTV Footage from ChokePoint

The evaluations above used simulated camera degradation, so to test the system on real surveillance video, the frozen backbone and the latest federated adapter, unchanged from the VGGFace2-HQ training, were evaluated on the ChokePoint dataset [@chokepoint]. ChokePoint was real CCTV footage of 29 people walking through two portals, filmed by three cameras above each portal at 800 × 600 pixels and 30 FPS, with every face annotated with its eye positions. Nothing was trained on ChokePoint, so the test measured how the system transferred to real cameras. The runs used an AWS GPU instance on 27 October 2026 and the scripts in `cctv_benchmark/`, and the protocol was the open-set protocol of Section 2.3.9 moved onto real footage. In each of 10 random splits, half of the people, 14 of them, were put on the watchlist and the others formed the crowd, and the two official sequence groups of the dataset were used in turn, one for enrolment and the other for the probes, so a person was always enrolled from a different walk-through than the one searched. Each watchlist entry averaged three frames, each probe fused three consecutive frames, the live system's threshold of 0.24 was used, and the results were averaged over the 10 splits in both directions, which gave 20 evaluations.

Enrolled people scored 0.67 to 0.72 on average against their own watchlist entry, and people in the crowd scored 0.13 to 0.14 against their best match, so at an FPIR of 1% between 99.6% and 99.9% of the enrolled people were found and correctly named. The federated adapter changed none of the identification figures by more than 0.001, it lowered the crowd false alarms at the live threshold slightly, from 4.4% to 4.0% on the official crops and from 4.6% to 4.2% on the full frames, and it lowered the average scores of both enrolled and crowd faces by about 0.02. On the full frames, 16.6% of the faces could not be landmark-aligned and were passed as plain crops, yet accuracy stayed above 99.5%.

The live pipeline was then tested end to end. The application's own components, namely SCRFD detection, ByteTrack tracking, the quality gate, the backbone with or without the adapter and three-of-five track voting, ran over 24 full camera sequences, 14,751 frames at the pipeline's sampling rate, and every alert was scored against the annotations. With the frozen backbone, the system raised a correct alert in 95.2% of the walk-throughs by an enrolled person, 1,988 of 2,088, typically 1.3 seconds after the person first appeared, it never named the wrong person, and 3.9% of the crowd walk-throughs, 88 of 2,232, raised an alert. With the federated adapter the detection rate, the absence of wrong-name alerts and the median time to alert were identical, while the crowd alerts rose slightly to 4.1%, 91 of 2,232, and the alerts on tracks that matched no annotated face fell from 4 to 0. The quality gate passed 4,000 of the 7,433 detected faces, which was 54%, and almost every rejection, 3,429 of 3,433, was a face too small to recognise reliably, so face size at the camera, rather than the matching step, was the main constraint on what the system could see. The whole video benchmark ran in 5.6 minutes on the GPU.

These results showed three things. The system worked on real surveillance footage, finding almost every enrolled person and never confusing identities. The live threshold had to be set for each deployment, because the threshold of 0.24 had been chosen on the demonstration videos and let about 4% of the crowd raise alerts on ChokePoint, while a threshold set for an FPIR of 1% would still have found 99.6% or more of the enrolled people. The adapter changed little on real CCTV either, which matched its small effect on the simulated data. ChokePoint was, however, easier than a real missing-person search, since people walked towards the cameras, the watchlist was small at 14 people and the enrolment frames came from the same cameras rather than from a family photograph, so the near-perfect recognition figures could not be generalised, and the large VGGFace2-HQ evaluation remained the main measure of accuracy.

## 4.10 Discussion and Limitations

The results supported four claims and qualified one. A privacy-preserving missing-person search system could be built end to end, and on real CCTV footage it alerted on 95.2% of enrolled walk-throughs, never with the wrong name. Federated training reached the accuracy of centralised training on pooled data, so keeping the data at the sites cost no accuracy. Federation was far better than training alone, since every node's local adapter lost accuracy against the frozen backbone while the federated adapter gained slightly, so collaboration was worth 1.1 rank-1 points and 3.0 TPIR points over local training. SA hid individual updates at no accuracy cost, and no membership leakage was detectable. The claim that was qualified was the gain of the adapter itself. On this data the federated adapter gave a small but consistent gain over the frozen ArcFace backbone, 0.5 points of TPIR at an FPIR of 1%, 1.0 point at 0.01% and 0.3 points of EER with rank-1 unchanged, and it was stable over 200 rounds, but it stayed below the 2-point bar set before the run. The answer to whether the backbone should be frozen or trained was therefore to freeze it, since it did almost all of the work, to add the federated adapter where its 1 MB per round was cheap, since it helped most on the worst cameras, and never to fine-tune at a single site alone, which lost accuracy.

The main limitations were as follows. The evaluation used simulated degradation, and the real-CCTV test of Section 4.9 covered only 29 people with enrolment frames from the same cameras. The clean images of VGGFace2-HQ had been restored by GFPGAN, which might have smoothed or altered fine facial detail, so the clean enrolment photographs were cleaner than many real case photographs. All results came from one random seed, without confidence intervals over seeds. The backbone had been trained by InsightFace on WebFace600K, and identity overlap with VGGFace2 was not checked, which might have made the frozen baseline somewhat optimistic. The scoring layer was fitted on pooled embeddings and was not in the live matcher, and the default cosine mode sent the enrolled embedding to each node, while template mode avoided this at a cost of 4.7 rank-1 points. Finally, SA had no recovery from a node dropping out, DP had no usable operating point at this scale, and the live threshold of 0.24 had been set on the demonstration videos and needed to be set again for each deployment.

The figures were measured on different test sets and were not directly comparable. The published works tested on IJB-B, IJB-C or the original images, where both faces of a pair were web photographs, while FedMPS tested every pair as a clean enrolment photograph against a CCTV-degraded probe whose face was 20 to 48 pixels wide, so its verification task was much harder, while its 691-person gallery made its identification task easier than IJB-C. In open-set identification, which was how a missing-person system was used, FedMPS came closest to the published work, since at an FPIR of 10⁻³ its TPIR of 0.836 was 3.6 points below FedFS, which tested on undegraded faces with the AUROC as its verification measure. Its EER of 11.8% lay within the 10.4% to 12.9% range that FedHide reported for its protected models on 64 × 64 faces, and its TAR at a FAR of 10⁻⁴ of 57.5% matched the per-client result of Kim, Kim and Teoh. It was, however, far below the centralised and privacy-preserving ResNet-50 models of Cao et al. and Ji et al., which were tested on high-quality IJB-C images, and the difference was the probe quality, as the clean-probe ceiling of 87.8% at the same point in {tab:main} showed.

The comparison was also meaningful in three further respects. On the cost of federation and privacy, Ji et al. lost 2.0 points of TAR to their privacy mechanism and FedHide lost 0.9 to 3.4 points of EER to protection, while in FedMPS federation cost nothing against centralised training and SA cost no accuracy at all, although DP was not usable at this scale, so FedMPS did not match the formal guarantees of DP-FedFace or Ji et al. On communication, the five works trained a whole backbone of millions of parameters, or exchanged class prototypes, across clients, while FedMPS sent 1.05 MB per update, 165 times less than its backbone, at the price of not adapting the backbone itself. On evaluation, only FedFS reported the open-set TPIR at a fixed FPIR and none tested on surveillance video, while FedMPS was evaluated as an open-set search under camera degradation and on real CCTV footage.
