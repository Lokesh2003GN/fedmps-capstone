# CHAPTER 5 | Conclusion and Future Work

This chapter summarised what the project achieved, including its negative findings, and listed the directions in which the work could be extended. Section 5.1 drew the conclusions of the project against the two objectives set in Chapter 1, with the measured results that supported each of them and the recommendation that followed from them on whether to freeze or train the backbone. Section 5.2 then listed the future work, ordered from the extensions that would most strengthen the evaluation to those that would be needed before a deployment at real camera sites.

## 5.1 Conclusion

This project built and evaluated FedMPS, a federated, privacy-preserving system for searching CCTV footage for a missing person, and both objectives set in Chapter 1 were met. The first objective was a federated multi-camera pipeline. Face detection with SCRFD, tracking with ByteTrack, a quality gate, five-point alignment, a frozen ArcFace backbone and track-level voting ran at each node, and a 263,424-parameter adapter on top of the backbone was trained by FL with FedProx across five nodes with non-IID camera conditions, while raw video, face crops and training data never left a node and each update was 1.05 MB instead of 174.4 MB. The second objective was a set of privacy mechanisms, each of them measured. Pairwise-masked SA produced exactly the same model as plain aggregation at 3.1% extra run time. Update-level DP was implemented with a Rényi DP accountant, but even the weakest noise, at ε = 252, destroyed the adapter, so DP had no usable setting at this scale. Cancelable templates, keyed per case and per node, worked end to end and cost 4.7 rank-1 points at 512 bits, and a corrected MIA on images held out of training found no leakage, with an AUC of 0.501.

The system was evaluated with open-set identification metrics on every image of VGGFace2-HQ, 628,699 images of 4,605 people, split 70 to 30 by identity, so that 3,223 people trained across the five nodes and 1,382 held-out people formed a 691-person watchlist and a crowd of 31,850 probes of people who were not on it. Federated training ran for 200 rounds and settled, with the last round reported and no round selection, and it reached a rank-1 of 0.9734 and a TPIR of 0.9107 at an FPIR of 1%, against 0.9733 and 0.9080 for centralised training on pooled data. On real CCTV footage from ChokePoint, with the latest adapter, the live pipeline alerted correctly on 95.2% of enrolled walk-throughs, never with the wrong name, and 99.6% or more of the enrolled people were found at an FPIR of 1%. The server and node consoles carried a case from enrolment, through scanning and human review, to a confirmed sighting, which was demonstrated with the latest model on a recorded university event video in which 6 of the 7 alerts were the enrolled person and the seventh was rejected by the operator. Every step was recorded in an audit log, and only a human-confirmed face crop ever reached the server.

The effect of the adapter was reported as plainly as the rest. Against the frozen ArcFace backbone, the federated adapter gained 0.53 points of TPIR at an FPIR of 1%, 1.04 points at 0.01% and 0.33 points of EER with rank-1 unchanged, a gain that was consistent across metrics, stable over 200 rounds and largest on the worst camera, but below the 2-point bar set before the run. Training alone was worse, since every node's local adapter fell below the frozen backbone, by 1.1 rank-1 points on average, so federation was worth 1.1 rank-1 points and 3.0 TPIR points over local training. The recommendation was therefore to keep the backbone frozen, to add the federated adapter where its 1 MB per round was cheap, and never to fine-tune at a single site alone. The difficulty lay in information lost to camera degradation, and the part that could be corrected, a rigid shift of the embeddings, was mostly captured by the scoring layer. Against five published works, FedMPS matched the published per-client and protected results on EER and on the TAR at a FAR of 10⁻⁴ despite its much harder CCTV probes, and stayed within 3.6 points of the open-set TPIR at an FPIR of 10⁻³. FedMPS therefore showed that camera sites could cooperate on a missing-person search without pooling footage or biometric data, at no accuracy cost relative to pooling, with human oversight and an audit trail on every disclosure, and it showed by measurement where the limits of the current design lay.

## 5.2 Future Work

Several directions would extend this work. The first was harder real footage, extending the real-CCTV evaluation to enrolment from ordinary photographs, to probes from cameras at several distances as in the SCface dataset and to larger watchlists, together with night-time and infrared footage, which might contain systematic distortions on which an adapter could help more. The second was per-site thresholds derived from each site's own DET curve, since the live threshold had been set on the demonstration videos. The third was repeated runs of every experiment over several seeds with confidence intervals, to resolve differences as small as those in {tab:main}, and the fourth was learning from operator decisions, feeding the confirmed and rejected alerts of each node, which were labelled examples from that camera, into the training rounds. The fifth was moving the scoring layer into the live matcher, with its statistics computed through SA, and the sixth was dropout-robust SA with Shamir-shared self-masks, so that aggregation survived a dropped node. The seventh was adopting the more accurate random-projection template scheme and making template-only matching the default, and the last was deployment and real-time operation, running the nodes and the server on separate machines with an edge GPU at each site for 8 FPS.
